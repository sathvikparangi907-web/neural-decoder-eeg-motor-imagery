Final-year project · the whole of it

Neural Decoder using EEG & Motor Imagery

Reading imagined movement from brain signals — and what happens when the decoder meets someone it has never seen before.

The short version. Every decoder we tested works reasonably well on a person it was trained on, and roughly 20–26 points worse on a stranger. The model we first designed to close that gap had the largest gap of all. Improved one measured change at a time, it now reaches 53.6% on a stranger at 3,924 parameters — smaller than EEGNet, and level with it rather than ahead of it.

The finding the project turns on: above 3,924 parameters, accuracy stops improving. A second encoder layer adds nothing and doubling the width adds nothing, so the gap that remains is not a capacity problem.

This page is the whole project: the problem, the recordings, how they are processed, the model, what it was compared against, the result, the evidence behind it, how it was measured, and what we got wrong. Results that have since been overtaken are kept near the foot of the page rather than deleted, and every change is dated in FINDINGS.md.

What the decoder is being asked to do

Nine volunteers wore a 22-electrode cap. An arrow told each of them which movement to imagine — never actually perform — and the brain activity was recorded. The decoder's job is to read the signal and name the movement.

Left handRight hand Both feetTongue

Four choices, so a decoder that simply guessed at random would be right 25% of the time. Every number on this page should be read against that floor: 30% is barely better than a coin toss with four sides, and 75% is a genuinely useful device.

For scale, the best published results for a stranger on this dataset sit in the low-to-high fifties — CTNet reports 58.6%, and re-implementations of EEGNet and EEG Conformer 56.9% and 53.4%. So the numbers here are ordinary for the task, and a long way from a usable device.

Small gaps are not results. With nine volunteers, a difference has to reach about 5.4 points before this test could reliably detect it. The one-to-four point gaps between the models below are therefore differences we cannot prove, and this page never claims otherwise.

The problem

It knows you, or it doesn't

The same decoder is tested two ways. The difference between them is the entire project.

Trained on you

77.3% Best model, tested on a second session from the same person it learned from. This is what a device does after you sit through a calibration.

Never met you

52.4% Best model, tested on someone whose data it has never seen. This is what a new user actually experiences when they put the cap on.

A 25-point collapse. Calibration is exactly the thing that makes these devices impractical — nobody wants to spend twenty minutes training a wheelchair before using it — so the right-hand number is the one that decides whether the technology is usable.

The recordings

BCI Competition IV, Dataset 2a

Nine volunteers, recorded at Graz University of Technology and distributed by BNCI Horizon 2020. Each sat two sessions on different days, which matters more than it sounds: a person's own signals shift between them, and the people whose signals shift most turn out to be the hardest to decode.

PropertyValue
Participants9  (A01–A09)
Sessions each2, on different days
Trials288 per session · 576 per person · 5,184
Classesleft hand, right hand, feet, tongue
Electrodes22 EEG  (plus 3 eye channels, discarded)
Sampling250 Hz
Analysis window0.5–4.0 s after the cue = 875 samples

A trial runs like this. A fixation cross and a beep; two seconds later an arrow pointing left, right, down or up, telling the volunteer which movement to imagine; they hold the imagery until the cross clears at six seconds. The window the decoder sees starts half a second after the arrow, which leaves out the brain's response to the arrow itself.

Trials are flagged, never deleted, when any channel swings past 100 µV — 4.3% on average, but 25% for A09, whose flags fell mostly on one class. The flag applies to training people only, so no test person is ever scored on a convenient subset of their own trials. The 18 raw files are in the repository with SHA-256 checksums, so every number here traces back to exact bytes.

Preprocessing

Eight steps, five of them cached

Five steps do not depend on which person is held out, so they run once. Three do, so they run again inside every one of the nine rounds.

#StepWhen
1Keep the 22 EEG channels, drop the 3 eye channelsonce
2Band-pass 4–38 Hz, zero phase, on the continuous recordingonce
3Cut each trial, 0.5–4.0 s after the cueonce
4Flag artefact trials above 100 µV peak to peakonce
5Cache each person as a 576 × 22 × 875 arrayonce
6Euclidean alignment: whiten each session by its own covarianceper round
7Standardise, using statistics from the training split aloneper round
8Augment the training split by recombining real segmentsper round

Two details carry more weight than they look. The band-pass runs over the continuous recording before trials are cut, so no filter ringing lands at a trial edge, and it runs in both directions, which cancels the phase shift that would otherwise smear the signal in time. Step 6 needs no labels at all, which is why it can be applied to a brand-new user.

The model

HCT-Net, 3,924 parameters

A convolutional front end borrowed from EEGNet, feeding one small Transformer encoder layer. The convolution's output width is the encoder's model dimension, so the two halves join with no projection layer between them, which is what makes the parameter count fall out exactly as designed.

StageWhat it doesOutputParams
Temporal conv8 filters across time — learned frequency bands8×22×875528
Spatial convone filter per band across all 22 electrodes; 22 become 116×1×875384
Separable convmixes the feature maps, then pools to 27 time steps16×27544
Windows5 overlapping windows of 11 steps, with a learned position code5×11×16176
Encoderone Transformer layer, two heads, shared by all five windows5×11×162,224
Headaverage over windows and time, then one linear layer468
Total3,924

The four sizes are named A to D. A, above, is the final model and the smallest. D is the size written into the design document before any experiment — 20,996 parameters, five times larger, with two encoder layers and double the width. B and C lie between. Only those two settings differ.

On a new person, the batch-normalisation statistics inside the convolutional part are recomputed from that person's unlabelled trials. No labels, no gradient steps, about 0.2 seconds of work. It is worth +4.3 points and improved all nine test people.

EEG Conformer lands 1.4 points below its published figure and FBCSP about 3, which was the bar we set ourselves before writing our own model. ATCNet and CTNet fall 18 points short, and we report that rather than averaging it away: their reference code trains for far longer than this protocol allows, so their numbers here may understate what those architectures can do.

Published figures are not all alike. The numbers usually quoted for these models come from different protocols — some within-subject, some with more training people, different windows and epoch budgets. They are here for reference, not as a like-for-like ranking.

That last row is the clearest single demonstration on this page: the same classical method, with and without re-centring each session, goes from 30.2% to 47.1%. Nothing about the classifier changed — only the alignment of the input.

EEGNet is 0.7 points ahead on validation, inside our own 0.75-point decision threshold, so this is a tie and is reported as one. Measured on the test people, the gap is −0.7 points with a 95% confidence interval from −4.3 to +2.9 — an interval that comfortably contains zero, which is the statistical way of saying the same thing. The four deep baselines in the table above have not been re-run under this protocol, so they are not in it. What the sweep settled is that a second encoder layer and double the width each buy nothing: above 3,924 parameters the line is flat.

Why

The model was never the lever

Three separate measurements point the same way: what limits cross-subject decoding is not the size of the network, not its attention block, and not how well it does on people it has seen. Each is shown below.

Evidence, two of three

Bigger models buy almost nothing

For a stranger, the top three decoders finish within 1.2 points of each other — while differing in size by a factor of 219.

Size is trainable parameters — roughly, how many adjustable dials the model has. EEG Conformer carries 219 times as many as EEGNet and gains 0.9 of a point on a stranger. Whatever the extra capacity is buying, it is not generalisation.

Evidence, three of three

Taking our own model apart

HCT-Net combines a small signal-filtering front end with an attention block, and adds a preprocessing step that mathematically re-centres each person's signal onto common ground. It is deliberately small — 20,996 parameters, about a fifth of ATCNet.

On a familiar person it is excellent: second of six, beating EEGNet by 7.3 points. On a stranger it finishes fifth of six. To find out which part was responsible, each component was removed in turn and the whole evaluation repeated.

Only one component earns its place. Removing the signal re-centring costs 5.1 points, so it is doing real work — and it helps the classical decoder by a similar amount, so the effect is not specific to one architecture. Removing the attention block makes the model better, which is awkward, because attention is the component the model is named after. Depth made no measurable difference at 1, 2 or 6 layers.

And a trap we fell into

Doing well on a familiar person predicts nothing

Corrected 7 November 2026. This section used to report r = −0.86 between familiar-person skill and the transfer loss, and called it the project's strongest result. That correlation is an artefact: the loss is computed from the familiar-person score, so the two share a term and must correlate. The honest test relates the two accuracies directly, and at six models it settles nothing either way.

r = 0.08   familiar-person against stranger accuracy, six models (95% CI −0.78 to 0.84, p = 0.88) — no evidence either way

The explanation is that a powerful decoder spends its capacity learning things that are true of the individuals it trained on — skull thickness, exactly where the cap sat, that person's particular way of imagining a movement — rather than things true of imagined movement in general. The better it gets at that, the less of it survives the move to a new head.

That story may still be true — the size sweep and the component study both point at it — but this correlation is not evidence for it, and six models cannot settle it. Reproduce both figures with py paper/extra_analyses.py.

How it was measured

The rules, and what they cost us

Everything above depends on the measurement being trustworthy, so the protocol was fixed in advance and then corrected once, in public.

  • Three roles, three people. In each of the nine rounds, seven people train the model, an eighth decides which version to keep, and the ninth is scored once at the end and never influences a choice.
  • Stopping and choosing were separated. Under the first protocol one person did both jobs, and a model stopped on someone is already fitted to them. A tenth of the training people's trials now decides when to stop.
  • The noise floor was measured before anything was trusted. The same configuration, three seeds, nothing else changed, moves the nine-round average by 0.9 points. So a single-run gain under about 1.3 points cannot be told apart from the random seed.
  • Hence the threshold. Three seeds are the unit of evidence and a change is kept only if it improves the validation average by 0.75 points or more.

It cost us two results of our own: two changes accepted earlier on single runs sat inside that noise band and did not survive being measured again. Both were dropped.

What is actually ours

Nothing in the architecture, and that is the point

Every block of this model came from a published one, and the repository names the paper each came from with the code beside it. The depthwise front end and the max-norm constraint are EEGNet's. The encoder layer is the 2017 Transformer. Putting convolution before attention follows EEG Conformer and CTNet. The windowed attention came from ATCNet, the augmentation from EEG Conformer and CTNet, the alignment from He and Wu, and the test-time adaptation is the no-gradient half of TENT.

BorrowedFromWhat measurement said
Depthwise–separable front endEEGNetkept
Transformer encoder layerVaswani et al.kept, cut to one

Batch-norm re-estimationTENT, its no-gradient halfkept, +3.5

Six encoder layersCTNetcut to one
Full TENT, entropy minimisationTENTrejected

So the contribution is not a new block. It is three things that the papers above did not do:

  • A protocol strict enough that a refutation can be believed — three roles held by three different people, a measured noise floor, and a threshold derived from it rather than chosen to suit.
  • Three ways of improving the model, tested and refuted under that protocol: more capacity, the attention block, and making more of the network adaptable. The third was our own idea, built at an identical parameter count, and it was worse on every one of the nine people.
  • The finding that is left: every point of cross-subject gain came from normalising distributions, and none from the model.

Said plainly, because it is the question an examiner asks first: this model does not beat EEGNet, and the deck and the paper both say so. What it does is make the comparison honest enough that the negative results mean something.

Being straight about it

What this does and doesn't show

  • Our model ties, it does not win. As first designed it was significantly worse than ATCNet. After the improvements it sits within half a point of the leader, and no difference among the top five is statistically reliable. Under the current protocol EEGNet is still 0.7 points ahead of us on validation.
  • Size turned out not to be the lever. Shrinking the model from 20,996 parameters to 3,924 narrowed the familiar-to-stranger gap from 24.7 points to 22.8, or 20.6 to 18.5 with adaptation. The ~18 points that survive at every size tested are not a capacity problem and will not be fixed by shrinking further.
  • One improvement was not chosen blind. Attending over the whole trial was tried because an earlier experiment had already shown it winning on the test people. It was then confirmed on the separate validation people, but its test result had been seen first.
  • The design's reasoning about windowed attention was wrong. Attending over the whole trial beat it twice, so that part of the design document has to be rewritten rather than defended.
  • Nine people is a small sample. Several real effects — including the 5.1-point value of signal re-centring — point consistently in one direction without reaching statistical significance, because with nine subjects and two poor performers the test simply cannot clear the bar. That is a limit of the dataset, not evidence the effects are absent.
  • Two baselines fell short of their published accuracy by around 18 points under our protocol. Their comparison is still fair — every model ran under identical conditions — but their numbers should not be presented as matching the original authors'.
  • The baselines ran with a single random seed. Only our final model averages three. How much a single-seed result moves between runs was not measured properly, so small gaps between baselines should not be over-read.
What would be worth doing next

Measure the final model on familiar people too, so its familiar-to-stranger drop can be compared with the original 26.3 points. Run the baselines with three seeds each, so the comparison with our three-copy model is like for like. And test whether the signal re-centring step is what drags ATCNet down on the three hardest people — the one explanation for its shortfall that was not ruled out.
