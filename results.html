Final-year project · School of Computer Science and Engineering, VIT-AP

Neural Decoder using EEG & Motor Imagery

Reading four imagined movements from scalp EEG, and testing it on people the decoder has never met.

Abstract. A motor-imagery decoder normally has to be calibrated on each new user before it works. We ask whether the usual remedy — more capacity, more attention — helps at all on an unseen person. Six decoders from 3,188 to 697,412 parameters were run on BCI Competition IV Dataset 2a under one protocol, nine rounds, each testing on a person held out entirely. Accuracy did not grow with size. Our own model, HCT-Net, reaches 53.6% on an unseen person at 3,924 parameters (chance 25%), level with EEGNet rather than ahead of it. Of five interventions tested, the three that changed the model failed and the two that normalised the data worked. Every point of cross-subject gain came from normalising distributions, none from the model.

Introduction

A brain–computer interface lets someone act through recorded brain activity rather than muscles. In motor imagery the user mentally rehearses a movement — a hand, the feet, the tongue — and the decoder names it from the rhythms this produces over the sensorimotor cortex. The people it would help most are those who cannot use a keyboard, a touchscreen or their voice.

The obstacle is calibration. A decoder trained on you works for you and poorly for everyone else, so each new user must first record a labelled session. CTNet, one of the models compared here, reports 82.52% on a person it trained on and 58.64% on one it did not: a drop of 23.88 points. Only the second number describes anyone's first use of the device.

Recent decoders answer with scale — attention layers, hundreds of thousands of parameters — but those gains are almost always measured within a person. This project asks one question: on a small public dataset, does extra capacity make a decoder better at people it has never seen?

How to read the numbers. Four classes, so guessing scores 25%. Published cross-subject results on this dataset sit in the low-to-high fifties. With nine volunteers, a difference must reach about 5.4 points before this test can reliably detect it — so the one- to four-point gaps below are not differences anyone can prove, and this page never claims otherwise.

2

Data

BCI Competition IV Dataset 2a, recorded at Graz University of Technology. Nine volunteers, each in two sessions on different days — a detail that matters, because a person's own signals shift between sessions and those who shift most are hardest to decode.

PropertyValue
Participants · sessions9 · 2 each, different days
Trials288 per session · 576 each · 5,184
Classesleft hand, right hand, feet, tongue
Electrodes · rate22 EEG · 250 Hz
Window analysed0.5–4.0 s after the cue = 875 samples

Each trial shows a fixation cross and a beep, then an arrow two seconds later telling the volunteer which movement to imagine; they hold it until the cross clears at six seconds. The decoder sees from half a second after the arrow, which excludes the brain's response to the arrow itself. Trials above 100 µV peak to peak are flagged, not deleted — 4.3% on average, 25% for A09 — and the flag is applied to training people only, so no test person is scored on a convenient subset.

3

Method

3.1  Preprocessing

Five steps run once and are cached: keep the 22 EEG channels, band-pass 4–38 Hz, cut the trial window, flag artefacts, store. Three depend on who is held out and run inside every round: Euclidean alignment (whiten each session by its own covariance), standardisation using training-split statistics only, and augmentation of the training split by recombining real segments of the same class. The band-pass runs over the continuous recording in both directions, so no filter ringing lands at a trial edge and nothing is smeared in time. Alignment needs no labels, which is why it can be applied to a brand-new user.

3.2  The model

HCT-Net joins a convolutional front end modelled on EEGNet to one small Transformer encoder layer. Eight temporal filters act as learned frequency bands; a depthwise convolution gives each one a spatial filter across all 22 electrodes, collapsing them to one; a separable convolution mixes the result and pools it to 27 time steps. Those are cut into five overlapping windows of eleven steps and passed through a single two-head encoder, then averaged and classified. The convolution's output width is the encoder's model dimension, so the halves join with no projection layer.

1,456 parameters in the convolutions, 2,224 in the encoder, 176 in the position code, 68 in the classifier — 3,924 in all. Four sizes were compared: A is this one, the smallest and the final model; D is the size written into the design document before any experiment, 20,996 parameters. On a new person the batch-normalisation statistics are recomputed from their unlabelled trials: no labels, no gradients, about 0.2 s.

3.3  What it was compared against

FBCSP with LDA, reimplemented, and four published networks from braindecode at their authors' defaults: EEGNet (3,188 parameters), ATCNet (113,732), CTNet (152,364) and EEG Conformer (697,412). Two classical Riemannian methods were added later. Using the authors' own code means a shortfall is a real difference under this protocol, not a bug in our copy.

Checked against published within-subject figures, EEG Conformer came 1.4 points short and FBCSP about 3, which was the bar set in advance before writing our own model. ATCNet and CTNet fell 18 points short; their reference code trains far longer than this protocol allows, so their results here may understate those architectures.

3.4  Protocol

Nine rounds. In each, seven people train the model, an eighth decides which version to keep, and the ninth is scored once and never influences a choice. Early stopping uses a tenth of the training people's trials, never the deciding person — a model stopped on someone is already fitted to them.

Before trusting any improvement we measured the noise: the same configuration, three seeds, nothing else changed, moves the nine-round average by 0.9 points. So a single-run gain under about 1.3 points cannot be told apart from the random seed. Three seeds are therefore the unit of evidence, and a change is kept only if it improves the validation average by 0.75 points or more.

It cost us two results of our own: two changes accepted earlier on single runs sat inside that noise band and did not survive being measured again.

The spread between people dwarfs every difference between models in this study. A device built on this would serve about a third of users well and a third badly, which is why nothing here is reported as an average alone.

5

Discussion

Three measurements point the same way. Capacity does nothing: four sizes of our model, from 3,924 to 20,996 parameters, finish within a point of each other, and across published decoders EEG Conformer carries 219 times EEGNet's parameters for nine tenths of a point. The attention block does nothing: removing it improved cross-subject accuracy, and global attention beat the windowed design the project was built around. Making more of the network adaptable made it worse.

What did work were two operations on the data rather than the model, both label-free: aligning each recording's covariance, and recomputing normalisation statistics on the new person. Alignment alone takes the classical method from 30.2% to 47.1%, and halves how often a trial's own participant can be identified from its channel variances, from 46.8% to 22.9%.

The reading we take from this is that the cross-subject gap is a problem of distribution, not of capacity, architecture or adaptability — and that within-subject accuracy is a poor guide to it. Across six decoders the correlation between doing well on a familiar person and doing well on a stranger is r = 0.08, which settles nothing at six models but certainly offers no support for the usual assumption.

An earlier version of this page reported r = −0.86 between familiar-person skill and the size of the drop, and called it the project's strongest result. It was an artefact: the drop is computed from the familiar-person score, so the two must correlate. It was withdrawn on 7 November 2026.

6

Limitations

  • Nine people, one dataset. Differences of a few points cannot be resolved at this size, and nothing here is shown to carry to other montages or populations.
  • We do not beat the baseline. EEGNet is 0.7 points ahead on validation. Inside our threshold, so a tie — but a tie, not a win.
  • The baselines were not treated equally. ATCNet, CTNet, EEG Conformer and FBCSP ran under the first protocol with one seed and no adaptation; only EEGNet and HCT-Net ran under the current one. Comparisons across that line indicate scale, not ranking.
  • Two baselines under-reproduced their published within-subject accuracy by about 18 points under our shared training budget, so their cross-subject numbers may understate them.
  • One ordering was not blind. The order in which two experiments were tried was influenced by a test result already seen. The keep-or-reject decisions used validation only, and the report says so.
  • A planned experiment is unfinished: giving each model its own analysis window, since some published models use 4–4.5 s against our 3.5 s.
