Phase 3 · Results

Neural Decoder using EEG & Motor Imagery

Reading imagined movement from brain signals — and what happens when the decoder meets someone it has never seen before.

The short version. Every decoder we tested works reasonably well on a person it was trained on, and roughly 20–26 points worse on a stranger. The model we designed to close that gap has the largest gap of all. That is a real finding, and it is the one worth reporting.

What the machine is doing

Guessing which movement you imagined

Nine volunteers wore a 22-electrode cap. An arrow told each of them which movement to imagine — never actually perform — and the brain activity was recorded. The decoder's job is to read the signal and name the movement.

Left handRight hand Both feetTongue

Four choices, so a decoder that simply guessed at random would be right 25% of the time. Every number on this page should be read against that floor: 30% is barely better than a coin toss with four sides, and 75% is a genuinely useful device.

The problem

It knows you, or it doesn't

The same decoder is tested two ways. The difference between them is the entire project.

Trained on you

77.3% Best model, tested on a second session from the same person it learned from. This is what a device does after you sit through a calibration.

Never met you

52.4% Best model, tested on someone whose data it has never seen. This is what a new user actually experiences when they put the cap on.

A 25-point collapse. Calibration is exactly the thing that makes these devices impractical — nobody wants to spend twenty minutes training a wheelchair before using it — so the right-hand number is the one that decides whether the technology is usable.

The finding

Learning a person is not the same as learning the task

Ranking the six decoders by how well they know a familiar person, then by how much they lose on a stranger, gives almost exactly the reverse order. Measured properly, the relationship is strong and statistically significant.

r = −0.86   correlation between familiar-person skill and transfer loss (p = 0.026, six models)

The explanation is that a powerful decoder spends its capacity learning things that are true of the individuals it trained on — skull thickness, exactly where the cap sat, that person's particular way of imagining a movement — rather than things true of imagined movement in general. The better it gets at that, the less of it survives the move to a new head.

This was predicted and recorded before the last two models were run, then held when they were. It is the project's strongest result.

A second finding

Bigger models buy almost nothing

For a stranger, the top three decoders finish within 1.2 points of each other — while differing in size by a factor of 219.

Size is trainable parameters — roughly, how many adjustable dials the model has. EEG Conformer carries 219 times as many as EEGNet and gains 0.9 of a point on a stranger. Whatever the extra capacity is buying, it is not generalisation.

The pale line marks the 25% random-guessing floor. A02, A05 and A06 sit close to it no matter which decoder is used. This is well documented and is a property of the people, not the software: some produce clear, repeatable brain rhythms when they imagine movement and some do not. It is why results are always reported per person — an average alone would hide that a third of users would get very little out of the device.

Our model

What we built, and what went wrong

HCT-Net combines a small signal-filtering front end with an attention block, and adds a preprocessing step that mathematically re-centres each person's signal onto common ground. It is deliberately small — 20,996 parameters, about a fifth of ATCNet.

On a familiar person it is excellent: second of six, beating EEGNet by 7.3 points. On a stranger it finishes fifth of six. To find out which part was responsible, each component was removed in turn and the whole evaluation repeated.

Only one component earns its place. Removing the signal re-centring costs 5.1 points, so it is doing real work — and it helps the classical decoder by a similar amount, so the effect is not specific to one architecture. Removing the attention block makes the model better, which is awkward, because attention is the component the model is named after. Depth made no measurable difference at 1, 2 or 6 layers.

Being straight about it

What this does and doesn't show

  • The proposed model did not win. It is significantly worse than ATCNet on a stranger, and no better than the 2008 classical method. No performance claim was made before the experiments ran, which is what makes this reportable rather than a failure.
  • Nine people is a small sample. Several real effects — including the 5.1-point value of signal re-centring — point consistently in one direction without reaching statistical significance, because with nine subjects and two poor performers the test simply cannot clear the bar. That is a limit of the dataset, not evidence the effects are absent.
  • Two baselines fell short of their published accuracy by around 18 points under our protocol. Their comparison is still fair — every model ran under identical conditions — but their numbers should not be presented as matching the original authors'.
  • Results use a single random seed. Re-running an identical configuration moved one result by 1.4 points, so differences smaller than that should be treated as noise — which includes the gaps between the top three decoders.
What would be worth doing next

The evidence points at a decoder that keeps the signal re-centring, drops the windowed attention, and stays small. An experiment now running trains the model to be actively unable to identify which person a signal came from, which attacks the failure these results identify rather than guessing at the architecture. Running each configuration with three seeds instead of one would also let the close comparisons be settled properly.
